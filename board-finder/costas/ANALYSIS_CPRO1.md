# Rosin's CPro1 (arXiv:2505.23881) read for what it says about Costas arrays

**Written 30 Sep 2026, before any Costas work is commissioned.** The operator's question was whether
frontier-model progress since mid-2025 makes Costas arrays worth another attempt. This document reads
the one paper that actually pointed an LLM-driven search system at Costas arrays and reports what it
got. The action plan is in `PLAN_COSTAS.md`; the structural computations it rests on are in
`costas_support.py` with output in `VERIFIED_OUTPUT.txt`.

**Tier note.** The paper's content below is `SECONDARY-RELAYED` — the operator supplied a transcript
and I read it. I did not fetch the PDF myself. Every number attributed to the paper is therefore
relayed at one hop, and I say so rather than implying I opened it.

---

## 1. What CPro1 is, stated precisely enough to reason about its budget

CPro1 takes a combinatorial design type, a formal verifier for it, and asks an LLM to write *search
heuristics* — not to output objects. Per design type the protocol is:

| stage | budget |
|---|---|
| candidate generation | **1000 programs**, as 50 ideas × 20 implementations |
| hyperparameter tuning | grid search over each surviving candidate |
| optimisation of the best | **top 5** carried forward |
| time per development instance | **2 hours** |
| time per open instance | **48 hours** |
| wall-clock per design type | **~6–10 days** |

It was run on **16 design types** from the *Handbook of Combinatorial Designs*. **Costas Array is one
of the 16** — it appears in the paper's Table 2 list of problem types, and its formal definition is
given in Table 5.

## 2. What it achieved, and the shape of the achievement

CPro1 **solved open instances for 7 of the 16 design types.** Costas Array is **not among the 7** —
it is absent from Table 1(a), the table of open instances closed.

The paper's own summary of the pattern, quoted:

> *"code generated by CPro1 solves many development instances, but no open instances"* — for the
> types that failed.

And §5's diagnosis, which is the single most useful sentence in the paper for our purposes:

> *"we don't see success with combinatorial design problems that have already seen sustained
> iterative development of computational methods by the research community (e.g. Covering Arrays)
> … we do see strong results in design types that have seen less attention."*

**This is a crowding claim, and it is the same observable this project's S4 gate is built on — except
that CPro1 states it as a predictor of its own failure.** Where a design type has a literature of
purpose-built computational attack, LLM-written heuristics did not beat it. Where the type had been
neglected, they did.

## 3. The methods that worked, and they are not exotic

The successful generated strategies named in the paper are **Tabu search, depth-first search with
backtracking, GRASP, constrained simulated annealing, and two-phase methods.** This matters in two
opposite directions and both belong in the record:

* **In favour of a retry.** None of these is beyond what a current model writes fluently, so the
  *ceiling* CPro1 measured was not the model's coding ability in 2025 — it was the interaction of a
  fixed 1000-candidate budget with a particular problem's difficulty.
* **Against a retry.** The published state of the art for Costas array enumeration **is** backtracking
  depth-first search with pruning. So on Costas specifically, the LLM's best available idea and the
  community's best method are *the same algorithm*, and the community's version has decades of
  tuning and (for the order-29 enumeration) a reported **366.55 CPU-years** behind it. There is no
  algorithmic gap for a model to discover; there is a constant-factor and compute gap.

That second reading is why Costas sits exactly in §5's failure class rather than its success class,
and it is not a budget artefact.

## 4. The one place the paper diagnoses a pure scale failure

On cap sets the paper is explicit that the budget, not the method, was binding:

> *"CPro1's run generated only 1000 candidate programs, FunSearch's 512 result was found in 4 of 140
> runs, each of which generated 2.5 million candidate programs."*

That is a factor of ~350,000 in candidate programs per run, and ~10⁸ overall. **Where a cell's known
solution required that much search, CPro1's 1000 candidates were never going to find it, and the
paper says so.** This is the honest reading of most of CPro1's nine failures: the protocol is a
*breadth* instrument across 16 design types, not a *depth* instrument on one.

**So "CPro1 failed on Costas" supports two incompatible conclusions** and the paper alone cannot
separate them:

| reading | what it implies for us |
|---|---|
| **budget failure** (the cap-set pattern) | a depth run on one design type, with 100× the candidates, is a genuinely untried experiment |
| **crowded-problem failure** (§5's own pattern) | the cell is hard for a structural reason that more candidates do not touch |

§5 assigns Costas to the second class by name-adjacency (it is a long-studied design type with
purpose-built computational attack). I think that assignment is right, and §7 says what would
change my mind.

## 5. A deficiency in CPro1's input that is fixable and is not the model's fault

**Table 5 gives the Costas array definition in the raw permutation formulation only** — a permutation
π of [n] such that the vectors (j−i, π(j)−π(i)) are all distinct. Nothing about the **Welch** or
**Golomb** algebraic families, nothing about sporadic arrays, nothing about corner deletion.

That is the correct formal specification and a terrible search brief. Every array known above order
~25 comes from an algebraic family or is a documented sporadic, and the structure of the known
solutions is exactly what a heuristic search should be exploiting. **A system handed only the
verifier is being asked to rediscover 60 years of construction theory inside a 48-hour run.**

This is the one deficiency in CPro1's Costas attempt that a retry could straightforwardly repair,
and it is why the attempt is not conclusive evidence about model capability. It is also, note, the
*only* one I can identify — the budget criticism applies to CPro1 as a protocol, not to Costas
specifically.

## 6. What I verified locally, because it decides the plan

`costas_support.py` builds the Welch and Golomb generators, checks every array it produces against an
independent `is_costas()` (with a positive control and two negative tests, one of which caught a bad
example of my own), derives the variant families by **corner deletion** rather than by recalling
their conditions, and measures how far those chains descend. Full output in `VERIFIED_OUTPUT.txt`.
`PRIMARY-VERIFIED-LOCALLY`:

1. **Corner-deletion chains are short** — the deepest measured over all primes below 60 is **4**
   steps. So a base array of order *B* covers orders *B* down to *B*−4 and no lower.
2. **Orders 31, 32 and 33 receive zero arrays from any Welch or Golomb generator, at any deletion
   depth.** The gap is caused by the prime gap 31 → 37: there is **no prime in [32,36] and no prime
   power in [33,36]**, so Welch (base *p*−1) and Golomb (base *q*−2) both step over the block.
3. **That conclusion does not depend on the fields I did not build.** For *n* = 31, 32, 33 every
   field that could nominally reach the order at depth ≤ 4 is a **prime** — 37 — which *was* built,
   and produced nothing. GF(2⁵) tops out at order 30 and cannot reach upward. This is asserted in
   PART 6 and the assertion passes.
4. **log2(32!) = 117.66**, so with 8 solutions assumed `bits_search ≈ 114.7` — the same order as
   Hadamard 2092 at 2⁻¹⁴¹ and the *v* = 487 positive control at 2⁻¹³².
5. **Order 31 is in the same uncovered block and has ≥ 8 known arrays** (relayed, Drakakis 2011, via
   this project's own `../gap/discovery/RESULT_B2_SEQUENCES.md`). Since nothing algebraic reaches
   order 31, **those arrays are necessarily sporadic** — somebody found them by search.
   **log2(31!) = 112.66, and the gap to order 32 is exactly 5.00 bits.**

**Point 5 is the finding that makes this worth a phase 0 at all.** There is a directly comparable
positive control five bits away, on the same object, with the same absence of algebraic structure.

## 7. A model of C(n) I tried and am reporting as failed

I first estimated the number of Costas arrays at order *n* with a birthday model — *n*! times the
probability that the difference vectors at each column gap collide nowhere, treating each as uniform
on the 2*n*−2 available values. Calibrated against known counts for *n* = 16…29 the offset **drifts
monotonically from +1.3 to +13.6 bits, sd 4.13**, because the true counts are not monotone (C(26) =
56 but C(28) = 712). Extrapolated, it predicts E[C(32)] ≈ 0.0003 — i.e. **non-existence** — which is
exactly the conclusion a broken estimator should not be allowed to deliver, since the same model
understates C(29) = 164 by a factor of 13,000.

**Reported as a failure rather than calibrated into looking useful.** The family-coverage computation
in §6 replaced it, and is a structural argument rather than a fitted one.

## 8. What would change my reading of §4

I assign Costas to §5's crowded class. The evidence that would move it to the budget class is
specific and is what phase 0 in `PLAN_COSTAS.md` goes looking for:

* a **published structural characterisation of sporadic Costas arrays** that constrains the search
  space — if sporadics are known to be perturbations of algebraic arrays, or symmetric, or to have
  constrained shift sequences, then a search brief that encodes that is not the search CPro1 ran;
* evidence that the **order-31 sporadics were found heuristically** rather than by exhaustive or
  near-exhaustive search — that would put a working method 5 bits from the target;
* the absence of any report of a **heuristic search at order 32 having been run and failed**. This is
  debt **D51**'s three-state distinction and it is the decisive one: "nobody tried" and "somebody
  tried and failed" are different worlds and this project's crowding gate cannot tell them apart.

If phase 0 finds sporadics structurally uncharacterised **and** a failed heuristic attempt at order
32 on the record, then the cell is attempted-and-failed with no structural lever, and the correct
move is to stop before spending Opus on it.
