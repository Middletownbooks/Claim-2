## 2. Correction 1 — the height convention, settled

*"by the height of a solution we mean its largest element in absolute value."* For odd *n*
symmetric, *B* = −*A*, so *A* is already centred and **their height is exactly our H**. Letac I
has height 99 and its primitive centred form is *A* itself.

So the `evidence/A4_literature.json` ambiguity is resolved and the "diameter" reading is struck.
The ratio is 3500 ÷ our reach: **50× at the H = 70 now on file**, ~39× when the ladder lands.
Still inside A-4's unregistered band, so A-4 still declines to score — D44 stands unchanged.

## 3. Correction 2 — it is an algorithmic result, and I said it was not

`SIEVE.md` §6 concluded: *"we are probably not one clever trick short; we are six orders of
magnitude of compute short."* **That is wrong**, and the paper's §4.1 is why.

The sieve is not a filter there. It is the **generator**. Two facts do it:

1. *A* ≡ −*A* (mod *p*) for every *p* | *C′_n*, and *n* odd forces an **odd** number of elements
   divisible by *p* — so at least one, and we may take *p*₁ | *a*₁.
2. From (9), *C′_n*(*A*, −*A*) = ±∏ᵢ(*aᵢ* + *a_j*) **for every *j***. So for each *j* there is
   some *i* with *p* | (*aᵢ* + *a_j*).

Chaining (2) gives their selection rule (17): each newly chosen element is **forced into a
residue class** mod *p*₁ or *p*₂, so it has ≈2*H*/*p* candidates instead of 2*H*. Measured
against this project's enumeration:

| H | ours, C(H,6) | theirs, §4.1 before any *q*/*F* pruning | ratio |
|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| 90 | 622,614,630 | 11,818 | **52,684×** |
| 500 | 2.1 × 10¹³ | 1.9 × 10⁹ | 10,909× |
| 2000 | 8.8 × 10¹⁶ | 3.2 × 10¹³ | 2,790× |
| 3500 | 2.5 × 10¹⁸ | 1.6 × 10¹⁵ | 1,599× |

And that is *before* the checks mod *q* = 19, 17, 13, the precomputed moment table mod *r* = 23,
and the polynomial *F*₁₁ — which the paper prints in full, 21 terms in 5 variables, and which
reduces a 7-element partial selection to a single vanishing test evaluated mod 2⁵⁰ then mod
2³⁰+3.

So the frontier at 3500 is a **method** result. It is exactly the sieve the user pointed at, used
to drive the enumeration rather than to filter its output — which is the use `SIEVE.md` §5 named
as the one that pays, and then failed to imagine anyone had implemented.

## 4. And the divisor set I used was the weak one

`SIEVE.md` worked from Kleiman alone: 10! = 2⁸·3⁴·5²·7, so primes 2, 3, 5, 7. Table 1 of this
paper gives, for *n* = 11,

> *C*₁₁ divisible by **2⁸·3⁴·5³·7²·11·13·17·19**, and *C′*₁₁ additionally by **31**

— from Rees–Smyth's Multiplicity Lemma, Caley's thesis, and Filaseta–Markovich, none of which
were in the earlier transcript. Five more primes (11, 13, 17, 19, 31), and higher powers of 5
and 7. The measured 6,250× prune in `SIEVE.md` §3 was computed against the wrong, much weaker
divisor set; the real one is enormously stronger, and it is what makes (17) worth chaining.

They in fact use *p*₂ = 29, not 19, after separately checking the **unique** local solution mod
29, {1,1,1,15,19,21,21,22,23,25,25} — an upgrade that buys another factor of 29/19 per selection.

## 5. A-5 is falsified: the asymmetric region really is unswept

The paper searches **symmetric solutions only** — the abstract says so, §4.1 is the odd-*n*
symmetric algorithm, §4.2 the even one, and the *n* = 11 result is stated as *"no symmetric ideal
solutions for n = 11"* with height ≤ 3500. Borwein–Lisoněk–Percival likewise: *"no symmetric
ideal solutions with size n = 11 exist with height at most 2000."*

So `RESULT_GAP_v2.md` §6's claim that the asymmetric region is genuinely unswept **survives its
promoting search**, and A-5 — which predicted it would not — is **falsified**.

**And it is still not a target.** A general ideal solution at *n* = 11 is 22 integers under 10
moment conditions, less 2 for affine normalisation: **10 degrees of freedom against 6 for the
symmetric case**. At *H* = 100 that is 10²⁰ candidates. `PREREG_PTE11.md` §6 registered "if A-5
fails, the asymmetric region is the target" — that branch was written assuming *unswept* implies
*attackable*, and it does not. Recorded rather than quietly dropped.

## 6. What the paper leaves genuinely open, in its own words

Not recommendations — just what it says is not done, for the record:

- **Two of the eight imaginary quadratic UFDs are untouched.** *"we need to use one of
  Q(i√d) with d ∈ {1, 2, 3, 7, 11, 19, 43, 163}. We concentrate here on the first six."*
  **d = 43 and d = 163 are unsearched at every size**, and Algorithms 6.1 and 6.2 are published
  in full.
- **The number-field bounds are small**, and were run in SageMath: Table 5 ranges from *H* = 160
  to *H* = 2100. Solutions *were* found there — *n* = 9 in Z[i√2] and the Eisenstein integers,
  *n* = 10 and 12 in Z[i] — so the setting is not barren the way *n* = 11 over ℤ is.
- **Nothing at all over ℤ for *n* ≥ 17**, and for 13 ≤ *n* ≤ 16 the bounds are 2000, 900, 1100
  and 850.
- Five primes are conjectured but unproven as required divisors (Table 1, last column), including
  31 at *n* = 11 for the general constant and 29 at *n* = 12.

## 7. Where this leaves the verdict

`VERDICT_PTE11.md`'s conclusion — *n* = 11 over ℤ is not a target for this project — stands, and
is now better founded: their frontier rests on a divisor table and a chained generator this
project did not have, not on compute we could rent. The corrected reason is in §3 above, and
the ledger entry claiming otherwise has been amended.
